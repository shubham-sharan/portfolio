What this round is really testing

Culture and technical are done, so the technical bar is effectively cleared. A third and final round is almost certainly a product-judgment and decision round, most likely with the Head of Product or whoever owns the roadmap. If it is the Head of Product, they are judging one question: would I trust this person to own a slice of my roadmap without checking every decision?

That breaks into three things: are his product calls sound, will clients trust him, and will he work with product leadership rather than around it.

The risks to pre-empt, in order
  1. Every metric on the resume is an engineering metric. MRR +24%, CI time −40%, 0/106 hallucinations, none of them is a user or business outcome. A product interviewer will push straight past them to “what changed for the user or the business?”
  2. “MRR” is ambiguous. On the resume it means mean reciprocal rank. A product-minded interviewer may hear monthly recurring revenue. Say “mean reciprocal rank” out loud.
  3. No evidence of a customer conversation, or of saying no. Both are explicitly required by the JD and neither is on the resume. Have one real example of each ready, even a small one. Still needed from you.
  4. Gatestone is blank. Credit collection is regulated and PII-heavy, arguably the most VeriFast-like thing you’ve done. Have a two-sentence description ready.
  5. “5+ years” vs the resume. The dated roles add up to about four. Reconcile it or drop the claim, a sharp interviewer will do the arithmetic.
  6. MediCoord users. “Shipped to production” invites “who uses it, how many, what changed?” Have an honest framing ready. Still needed.
The lines to land

  2. Zero hallucinated facilities is a guardrail. The user outcome is whether people got coordinated care faster. A model metric is not a user outcome
  3. Zero in 106 still allows a true error rate near 3% at 95% confidence. Say the rule of three before they do

The toolkit

Frameworks and concepts

1FCS: Frame, Choose, Ship

The spine for your own stories and any live case. It should shape your thinking, not show up as a script, say "there are really three ways to solve this", never "Section 4: Options".

PhaseSectionThe question it answers
Frame1. ProblemWhat are we actually solving? State it without a solution in it.
2. WhyWhy this, and why now, over everything else on the roadmap?
3. UserWhose pain is sharpest, and what is the insight that is not obvious?
Choose4. OptionsThree genuinely different ways, at least one without AI.
5. BetsThe pick, and what you are cutting. A real cut has a cost.
Ship6. BuildThe smallest v1 that tests the bet, with success, guardrail, kill.
7. FutureWhere it breaks first, and where it goes next.

Inside Build, always two metrics and a kill condition. A success metric on its own is how you celebrate a number while the product gets worse. The guardrail is whatever the success metric could cannibalise, and the kill condition is agreed before launch so it is nobody's fault when it triggers.

2The North Star metric, and its guardrail

A count makes a strong North Star; a rate makes a strong guardrail. The North Star is the number that represents real value and can keep growing. The guardrail is the rate that stops you buying that growth by making the product worse.

VeriFast example, the support agent: the North Star is tickets resolved without a human and not reopened within seven days. The guardrail is CSAT on bot-handled tickets against the human baseline. The kill condition, agreed before launch, is CSAT dropping below baseline for two weeks in a row.

A North Star that can rise while quality falls is not enough on its own. That is why it always travels with a guardrail.

Check question

"Give me a North Star and a guardrail for document extraction."

3Prioritisation

Three questions, in order:

  1. Is it the constraint? Every system has one step that is the bottleneck. Improving the constraint raises total output; improving anything else just rearranges the queue.
  2. Is it hard to reverse? Reversible calls, like a prompt behind a flag, should be made fast. Hard-to-reverse ones, like a price, a compliance precedent, or data you never recorded, deserve more care.
  3. What would it cost to be wrong? This decides how much time to spend on the decision, not what you build.

State criteria before ranking: reach, cost per occurrence, confidence, reusability, reversibility. And some work should be dropped, not deferred, "deprioritised" is often a polite way of saying never.

Check question

"Rank these for next quarter and tell me what you are cutting: document reading, the support agent, a custom client feature."

4Spec writing

The shape: problem and evidence, users, goals and non-goals, success metric with guardrail and kill condition, v1 scope, risks, open questions. For an AI feature, add eval criteria, behaviour when the system is unsure, and the escalation policy.

Options is the section most specs skip, and the one that matters most. A junior spec describes an idea; a senior spec describes a choice. Listing the alternatives you rejected, and why, is what stops the argument three weeks into the build. Non-goals prevent scope creep and tell support and clients what not to expect.

5When, and when not, to use AI

Do not use AI when the answer is a rule (write code), when it must be exact (arithmetic, a lookup), or when variation is unacceptable. Use AI where judgement is required and some variation is acceptable.

VeriFast example, matching: a database query filters on skills and must-haves, those are rules, so they belong in code. The model then ranks the shortlist, which is judgement. The line runs through the middle of one feature: rules on one side, judgement on the other.

6Request vs the need

What a client asks for is usually their guess at a solution. The job is to find the problem underneath. Ask "why" until you reach the real pain, ask about what actually happened ("tell me about the last time this went wrong") rather than hypotheticals, and watch how people really work.

VeriFast example: "auto-approve applicants" is the request. "Our approvals are slow and we lose good renters" is the need, which a ranked review queue can meet without taking the decision away from the client.

7Evaluating an AI feature

  • Evals from real data. A set of real, messy examples with known answers, real past tickets and real blurry pay stubs, not clean made-up ones.
  • Which error matters more. Wrongly rejecting a good applicant hurts a renter and creates legal risk; wrongly accepting a fake document costs the client money. Decide which to tune against, and it can differ by client.
  • Offline is not online. Test scores and real results diverge, so keep sampling real conversations, human override rates and reopen rates after launch.

Your 0/106: know what it proves (the model did not invent facilities), what it does not (that users got better care), and why 106 clean tests still leave a true error rate near 3%.

8Phased autonomy

Do not hand the AI full control on day one. Raise its independence as it earns trust:

  1. Suggest, the AI drafts and a person decides.
  2. Approve, the AI prepares the reply and a person sends it.
  3. Automatic on proven cases, once a category hits the agreed accuracy.
  4. Always, a human takes over when the AI is unsure, with an instant off switch.

It matters because VeriFast is regulated, dealing with housing, income and identity, where one public mistake loses a client's trust. It is also how you say no: "not full autonomy yet, and here is the path."

9CIDER, the story spine

For telling any ownership story in about two minutes:

LetterWhat you say
ContextThe situation, and what was wrong.
InsightThe non-obvious thing you saw. ("Triage accuracy, not a nice map, was what earned trust.")
DecisionWhat you chose to do, and what you chose not to.
ExecutionHow you built and tested it, including what you cut.
ResultWhat happened, honestly, including what did not work.

CIDER is for a past story; FCS is for a live problem. They line up: Context and Insight are Frame, Decision is Choose, Execution and Result are Ship. Run MediCoord through CIDER, then a live VeriFast case through FCS, and you sound like the same product mind in both.

Part B · The VeriFast support agent case

6A property manager says: “Make the AI auto-approve applicants who pass verification.” What do you do?

Counter: “They insist. They're a big client.”

Better answer

“I'd ask why they want it. Usually the real problem is that approvals are slow, or they lose good renters to faster landlords. Auto-approval is risky because the decision legally belongs to them, not us. So I'd offer something faster that keeps them in control, like a ranked list of ready applicants with a one-page summary, so approving takes one click instead of twenty minutes. That fixes the real pain without taking on their risk.”

7Build an AI agent for applicant support tickets. You have two weeks. What do you do first?

Counter: “Why not just start building? You know the tools.”

Better answer

“First I'd read the last two or three months of tickets and sort them: which questions come up most, which take longest, and which are risky. Then I'd start with the common, low-risk ones, like 'Where's my verification?' or 'Which documents do you accept?' If I build before looking, I might automate the wrong thing.”

8What will the agent never do?

Counter: “I'm the applicant. Just tell me why I was rejected.”

Better answer

“It will never explain why someone was flagged or declined. Three reasons: it would teach fraudsters how to get around our checks, the decision belongs to the property manager, and there are legal rules about how rejections are communicated. It also checks who it's talking to before sharing any status. For that applicant, it would kindly explain that the property manager handles decisions and tell them how to reach them.”

9The client wants the agent fully automatic from day one.

Counter: “If you say no, they might walk.”

Better answer

“I wouldn't just say no. I'd say 'yes, in steps.' For two weeks the AI writes the replies and a person approves them. We track accuracy together. Once a type of question hits the target we agreed, that type goes fully automatic. They get automation fast, and neither of us is exposed to a public mistake.”

10Give me one number that tells me the agent is working.

Counter: “What could that number hide?”

Better answer

“The share of tickets solved without a human and not reopened within seven days. But that number alone can hide problems, since the bot could close tickets without really helping. So I'd also watch customer satisfaction, and make sure it doesn't drop below the human baseline. And before launch we'd agree on when to switch it off, for example if satisfaction drops for two weeks in a row, or if it ever shares private information.”

11Our tests said 95% accurate, but customer satisfaction dropped after launch. Why?

Counter: “How would you catch this earlier next time?”

Better answer

“The test questions probably didn't look like real ones. Real users write messy, emotional, or unusual messages. The bot might also have given answers that were correct but unhelpful, or failed to hand off tickets it should have escalated. To catch it earlier, I'd test on real past tickets, launch to a small group first, and review a sample of real conversations every week.”

Part C · Priorities, production, and integration

12Our biggest client wants a custom feature or they'll leave. But document reading is behind, and that affects every client. What do you do?

Counter: “The CEO says just build it for the client.”

Better answer

“I'd first ask the client what problem the custom feature solves. Often it can be solved with a setting or a small change to the main product, which helps every client. If it truly is one-off, I'd lay out the trade-off for leadership: what we'd delay, and whether other clients will ask for the same thing. If the CEO still decides to build it, I'll commit fully and write down the risks.”

13You built the agent in a week on Replit and the client loved the demo. What must be true before real applicants use it?

Counter: “Leadership says Replit is fine, ship Monday.”

Better answer

“Four things aren't negotiable: personal data is kept out of logs, a human takes over if the AI fails or is unsure, there's a switch to turn it off instantly, and it passes tests built from real tickets. If those are in place, I'm happy to ship Monday, but only to one ticket type or one client first.”

14Our system says the application is complete, but the client's property management system says it's still pending. What does the agent tell the applicant?

Counter: “Their system has no API at all.”

Better answer

“First I'd ask which system is the source of truth. If the two disagree, the agent shouldn't guess. It should say 'let me get someone to check this' and pass the ticket to a person. If their system has no API, I'd look at exports or reports, but I'd also ask whether this integration is worth building at all, or whether a clear handoff is good enough.”

15What would you do in your first 30 days?

Counter: “We need something shipped in 30 days, not just listening.”

Better answer

“Week one I'd read tickets, sit in on support and client calls, and learn the product. By week two I'd pick one small, painful problem. By week four I'd ship a small first version to a few real users, with a number we agreed on beforehand. So I listen first, but I still ship within the month.”
